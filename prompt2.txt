## 5. Literature domains

Organize the literature review by engineering topic, not by source filename.

Cover these domains:

### Domain LIT-01 — RFID fundamentals

Include:

- RFID system components;
- tag, reader, and controller roles;
- passive identification concepts;
- distinction between carrier frequency and credential-message format;
- security limitations of treating a card identifier as sufficient authentication.

Do not state that a specific protocol is implemented by the commercial card without product evidence.

### Domain LIT-02 — 125 kHz identification systems

Include:

- low-frequency RFID as a technology class;
- typical read-range and data-rate considerations only when supported;
- identifier-oriented credentials;
- limitations and security considerations;
- distinction between an RF technology and controller-side signaling.

Do not assign a specific tag standard to the commercial product unless supported.

### Domain LIT-03 — 13.56 MHz RFID and smart-card systems

Include:

- high-frequency RFID as a technology class;
- distinction between simple identifiers and smart-card protocols;
- possible cryptographic capabilities at the technology level;
- interoperability and implementation differences.

Do not claim that the commercial product supports MIFARE, ISO/IEC 14443, NFC, or another specific standard without evidence.

### Domain LIT-04 — Wiegand signaling and frame formats

Include:

- Wiegand as a reader-to-controller signaling convention;
- D0 and D1 pulse concepts;
- framing and parity principles;
- Wiegand-26 as a possible reference format for the later simulator;
- the difference between a proposed reference implementation and verified product support;
- protocol limitations and error handling.

If no appropriate authoritative Wiegand source is locally available, create an unresolved-source item rather than presenting unsupported details as established fact.

### Domain LIT-05 — Embedded-controller architecture

Include:

- processor, memory, peripherals, and firmware roles;
- memory-mapped I/O;
- initialization;
- timers;
- interrupts;
- serial interfaces;
- GPIO;
- watchdogs;
- reset and startup behavior;
- nonvolatile storage concepts;

Use ARM and STM32 documents as representative technical references, not as product-specific evidence.

### Domain LIT-06 — ARM concepts

- RISC load/store principles;
- general-purpose registers;

### Domain LIT-08 — Credential storage and authorization

### Domain LIT-09 — Elevator access-control integration

### Domain LIT-10 — Reliability and fault handling

## 7. Evidence and wording rules

Every planned technical claim must be classified as one of:

- `verified_product_evidence`
- `external_technical_evidence`
- `engineering_inference`
- `proposed_reference_design`
- `unknown_or_unresolved`

Do not allow a lower-authority source to replace a higher-authority source.

Examples:

- An ARM manual may explain memory-mapped I/O but cannot prove that the commercial card uses ARM.
- An STM32 manual may support a proposed reference architecture but cannot identify the product processor.
- A product photograph may show sixteen visible channels but may not prove their electrical ratings or circuit topology.
- A seller statement remains a seller statement unless independently verified.
- A simulation design decision remains a proposed reference design.

Use cautious wording such as:

- “The source defines...”
- “This project uses the concept as a representative reference...”
- “The commercial implementation remains unknown.”
- “The proposed simulator will model...”
- “This is an engineering inference rather than verified product evidence.”

Do not use wording such as:

- “The board uses STM32...”
- “The controller communicates through Wiegand-26...”
- “The outputs are relays rated for...”
- “The firmware stores...”
- “The product complies with...”

unless direct authoritative evidence exists.

## 11. Source-inspection method

Use a shallow-to-targeted process:

1. inspect title pages and tables of contents;
2. identify relevant chapters and sections;
3. extract only the needed sections;
4. confirm section scope;
5. paraphrase the relevant concept;
6. record precise source locations;
7. record limitations;
8. avoid recursively summarizing entire manuals.

Use available text extraction tools when they preserve source structure.

Do not use OCR unless the source lacks usable text and the exact information is necessary.

Do not repeatedly OCR large documents.

When diagrams or tables are essential and their content is not captured by text extraction, inspect the relevant page visually and record the page number and what the visual contributes.
